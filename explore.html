INT411 . Before we start Unit II

Go exploring

Unit II is big data inside real genomics. Before we meet, go and look at some of it yourself, and note what you find by hand.

This is not a test. It is a scouting trip. Pick up a few real facts, poke at a real tool or two, and most importantly, come back with questions. We will start the next class from your questions, not from my slides. Use any search engine, any website. The only rule: actually go and look.
1

How big is genomics, really?

Look up two numbers. First, roughly how much raw data does sequencing a single human genome produce? Second, find the size of one large public genomic archive (try searching the NCBI SRA, gnomAD, or 1000 Genomes).

Come back with: the two numbers, and which one surprised you more.

2

Getting the data from A to B

Imagine you must send 50 TB of sequencing reads to a lab overseas. Search why an ordinary download (FTP or HTTPS) is painfully slow at this scale. Then look up Aspera FASP and find the one trick it uses to go faster. If you have time, find one real project that shares scientific data using torrents.

Come back with: one reason normal transfer is slow, and one thing FASP does differently.

3

A genome is more than letters

Raw DNA is just A, C, G, T. Annotations tell you what the letters mean. Find out what a genome annotation is, then actually open one of Ensembl, the UCSC Genome Browser, dbSNP, or ClinVar and search for one gene or variant you are curious about.

Come back with: what you searched for, and one thing you saw on the page.

4

Where did this data come from?

Search for iRODS. In one line of your own, what problem does it solve for research data? Then think for yourself: in a clinical genomics study, why would it matter to know exactly which version of a dataset produced a result, and who touched it along the way?

Come back with: your one-line description, and your own reason versioning matters.

5

Your Unit I tools, on real genomes

You just learned MapReduce and Hadoop. Find one real bioinformatics tool that runs on Hadoop or Spark. Names worth searching: GATK on Spark, Hail, Crossbow, CloudBurst. This is Unit I meeting Unit II.

Come back with: the tool's name and, in one line, what it does.

6

Reading genes, not just DNA

DNA is the recipe book; RNA-seq tells you what is actually being cooked right now. Find out, in simple terms, what an RNA-seq experiment measures and what a gene expression matrix looks like.

Come back with: one question RNA-seq can answer that plain DNA sequencing cannot.

7

The healthcare side of big data

Unit II leans into genomics, but big data runs through healthcare too. Search for one real way hospitals or clinics use big data (some starting points: electronic health records, medical imaging AI, predicting patient readmission). Find one concrete example, not a definition.

Come back with: one real use, and whether you think it helps patients or mainly the hospital.

8

Formats you will actually meet

Genomics has its own file formats. Look up what these three are for: FASTQ, BAM, and VCF. In a sentence each, what does each one hold, and roughly in what order do they appear in a pipeline?

Come back with: one line on each, in the order they are produced.

9

Where genomic data lives

In Unit I we said big files get scanned and small facts get looked up. Search where large sequencing datasets are actually stored today (try terms like cloud genomics, AWS Open Data, or object storage). Why store terabytes in the cloud instead of a lab server?

Come back with: one place such data is stored, and one reason to keep it there.

10

The privacy question

Genomic and clinical data is deeply personal. Search for one rule or idea about keeping it safe (starting points: de-identification, HIPAA, GDPR, or federated analysis). What is one thing people do so data can be used for research without exposing patients?

Come back with: one method for protecting the data, in your own words.
